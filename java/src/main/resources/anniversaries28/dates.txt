2013:
Important technology introduced
Docker (March): made Linux containers easy to use and transformed how software is deployed.
React (May): Facebook open-sourced the UI library that came to dominate web front-end development.
iPhone 5s (September): brought Touch ID and the first 64-bit smartphone chip (A7). iOS 7 the same month introduced flat design.
PlayStation 4 and Xbox One (November): the start of the eighth console generation.
Chromecast (July), Google Glass Explorer Edition, and the Oculus Rift developer kit (March), an early step toward modern VR.
Intel Haswell processors (June), Windows 8.1 (October), and OS X Mavericks (October), the first free major macOS upgrade.
Ethereum whitepaper (late 2013): Vitalik Buterin’s proposal for programmable blockchains.
Bitcoin passed $1,000 for the first time (November).
Companies and services created
Telegram (August), Vine (January), and Robinhood.
Slack: the internal tool went into preview in August, with public launch in 2014.
Failures, endings and big business moves
The Snowden revelations (June) exposed mass NSA surveillance, including PRISM. They triggered a global shift toward encryption and privacy.
Google Reader was shut down (July). Lavabit, Snowden’s email provider, closed rather than hand over its keys (August).
Silk Road was shut down by the FBI (October).
BlackBerry 10 launched, but the company kept collapsing and reported a roughly $1 billion quarterly loss.
Healthcare.gov’s launch failure (October) became a textbook case of a failed government IT project.
Data breaches at Adobe (October) and Target (December, about 40 million cards). Yahoo’s massive breach also happened in 2013, though it wasn’t disclosed until 2016.
THQ was liquidated (January), and LucasArts was closed by Disney (April).
Big deals:
Microsoft agreed to buy Nokia’s phone business.
Dell went private.
Yahoo bought Tumblr.
Twitter went public.
Snapchat turned down a $3 billion offer from Facebook.
Steve Ballmer announced his retirement as Microsoft CEO.
Notable deaths
Aaron Swartz (January): co-author of RSS 1.0 and an early Reddit co-owner, he also worked on Creative Commons. He died at 26 while facing federal prosecution over downloading academic articles.
Douglas Engelbart (July): inventor of the computer mouse. His 1968 “Mother of All Demos” showed hypertext, windows, and video conferencing decades early.
Hiroshi Yamauchi (September): the president who turned Nintendo into a video game company.
Barnaby Jack (July): a security researcher famous for “jackpotting” ATMs on stage.
Ray Dolby (September): an audio-technology pioneer, slightly outside core IT.
Games released in 2013
Grand Theft Auto V (September): one of the best-selling and highest-grossing entertainment products of all time. GTA Online followed shortly after.
The Last of Us (June): Naughty Dog’s story-driven survival game, widely ranked among the greatest games ever made.

1943:
Important technology and ideas
Colossus was designed by Tommy Flowers in 1943 to break German Lorenz ciphers at Bletchley Park. The first one was working by the end of the year and used from early 1944, making it the first programmable electronic digital computer.
Heath Robinson (mid-1943): Colossus’s predecessor at Bletchley Park. It was an electromechanical and electronic codebreaking machine that proved the approach worked, but its paper tapes kept slipping.
ENIAC was commissioned when the US Army signed a contract with the University of Pennsylvania in June 1943. Mauchly and Eckert began building it, and it was completed in 1945.
Harvard Mark I (IBM ASCC) was largely built at IBM during 1943 and became operational at Harvard in 1944.
McCulloch and Pitts published the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron. It is the theoretical root of today’s neural networks and AI.
Wiener, Rosenblueth and Bigelow published “Behavior, Purpose and Teleology,” a founding text of what became cybernetics.
Alan Turing met Claude Shannon at Bell Labs in early 1943, during Turing’s visit to the US. The two founders of computer science and information theory reportedly discussed machine intelligence.
Losses
Konrad Zuse’s Z3, the world’s first working programmable computer (1941), was destroyed in an Allied bombing raid on Berlin in December 1943.
Companies

No major computer companies were founded or lost. The industry didn’t really exist yet, and IBM, the dominant player in punched-card machines, was busy with war production.

Notable deaths
Nikola Tesla (January): a pioneer of alternating current and much of the electrical engineering that computing later depended on.
David Hilbert (February): the mathematician whose “decision problem” (Entscheidungsproblem) led Turing to define the Turing machine in 1936. In a sense, he posed the question that invented computer science.
